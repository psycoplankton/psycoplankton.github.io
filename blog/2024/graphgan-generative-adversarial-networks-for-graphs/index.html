Now, Let’s jump onto the main topic of this article. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for Graphs.

But wait, generating graphs? Why?🤔. Generating text seems reasonable, generating images is understandable but graphs? Well, I thought this too, and the answer is very trivial. Graphs are just as important as texts or images. Let me give you an instance of where graphs are used for representing data. The protein sequence of a molecule is represented in the form of a graph network. Now imagine being able to generate protein molecules with absolutely new structures, that could cure cancer (okay, that is taking it a bit too far 😂) But really, think how easy it would become for researchers to produce new drugs if they could first get a vague idea of how the molecule should look, instead of experimenting again and again.

As we know, in a GAN architecture, the discriminator and the generator play a MinMax game between themselves. The generator learns from the gradient backpropagation of the discriminator loss and thus improves. Both generator and discriminator learn from each other and ultimately reach the equilibrium.

For graphs, the idea is the same, just the definitions and generator and discriminator change. Before going to the definitions, let’s define some terminologies so that you become comfortable with the rest of the mathematical concepts.

GraphGAN Framework

Let G = (V, E) be the given graph, where V = {v₁, …, vₙ } represents the set of vertices and E = {eₖₗ} where (i,j) goes from 1 to n represents the set of edges. For a given vertex νₙ, we define ℳ(νₙ) as the set of vertices directly connected to νₙ, the size of which is typically much smaller than the total number of vertices ν. We denote the underlying true connectivity distribution for vertex νₙas conditional probability Ť(ν|νₙ), which reflects νₙ connectivity preference distribution over all other vertices in V. From this point of view, ℳ(νₙ) can be seen as a set of observed samples drawn from Ť(ν|νₙ)

Generator

G(ν|νₙ; θg), which tries to approximate the underlying true connectivity distribution Ť(ν|νₙ), and generates (or selects, if more precise) the most likely vertices to be connected with νₙ from vertex set V.

Discriminator

D(ν, νₙ; θd), which aims to discriminate the connectivity for the vertex pair (ν, νₙ). D(v, vc; θd) outputs a single scalar representing the probability of an edge existing between ν and νₙ.

The optimal parameters of the generator and the discriminator can be learned by alternately maximizing and minimizing the value function V(G, D). In each iteration, discriminator D is trained with positive samples from Ť(ν|νₙ) (vertices in green) and negative samples from the generator G(ν|νₙ; θg) (vertices with blue stripes), and generator G is updated with policy gradient under the guidance of D. Competition between G and D drives both of them to improve their methods until G is indistinguishable from the true connectivity distribution. We discuss the implementation and optimization of D and G as follows.

Generator Optmization

In contrast to the discriminator, the generator aims to minimize the log probability that the discriminator correctly assigns negative labels to the samples generated by G. In other words, the generator shifts its approximated connectivity distribution (through its parameters θG) to increase the scores of its generated samples, as judged by D. A straightforward way is to define the generator as a softmax function over all other vertices. Softmax provides a concise and intuitive definition for the connectivity distribution in G, but it has two limitations in graph representation learning: 1) The calculation of softmax involves all vertices in the graph, which implies that for each generated sample ν, we need to calculate gradients ∇θg logG(ν|νₙ; θg) and update all vertices. This is computationally inefficient, especially for real-world, large-scale graphs with millions of vertices. 2) The graph structure encodes rich information of proximity among vertices, but Softmax completely ignores the utilization of structural information from graphs as it treats vertices without any discrimination. To address the aforementioned problems, in GraphGAN, we propose a new alternative to softmax for the generator called graph softmax.

We discuss graph softmax in detail as follows. To calculate the connectivity distribution G(ν|νₙ; θg), we first perform Breadth First Search (BFS) on the original graph G starting from vertex νₙ, which provides us with a BFS-tree Tₙ rooted at νₙ. Given Tₙ, we denote ℋₙ(ν) as the set of neighbours of ν (i.e., vertices that are directly connected to ν) in Tₙ, including its parent vertex and all child vertices if they exist.
